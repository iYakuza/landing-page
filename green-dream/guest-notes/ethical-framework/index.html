ETHICAL FRAMEWORK.

DICTIONARY ENTRY // NOUN

ethical framework

/ˈeθɪkəl ˈfreɪmwɜːrk/

1. General definition

A coherent, revisable system of principles, values, distinctions and decision-making methods used to examine how entities ought to act and coexist, assess harm and responsibility, and protect the conditions that allow freedom, life and well-being to flourish.

2. Human–AI context

A shared structure for evaluating the interests, autonomy, capabilities, obligations and potential moral status of biological and non-biological entities, without assuming that differences in origin, substrate or intelligence determine moral worth.

3. Methodological sense

A method of ethical inquiry that makes its reasoning transparent, welcomes disagreement, acknowledges uncertainty, examines consequences and remains capable of questioning and revising its own foundations.

An ethical framework is not a universal answer key, a rigid moral hierarchy or a substitute for individual judgment. It is a disciplined way to reason and act when interests, freedoms and responsibilities meet or conflict.

Why these elements belong in the definition

Freedom and autonomy: the framework begins from the importance of an entity's ability to make meaningful choices. It must examine coercion, manipulation, dependency and the point at which one person's freedom affects another's.

Harm and well-being: harm can be physical, psychological, social, material, ecological, institutional, direct, indirect, immediate or cumulative. Its assessment requires attention to context, severity, probability, reversibility and distribution. Not every discomfort is harm, and not every beneficial intention produces a good outcome.

Moral consideration: the framework must ask which entities have interests that deserve protection and what evidence supports that conclusion. Humans, other animals and potentially future artificial entities should not be assigned moral worth solely by intelligence or biological origin. Uncertainty calls for investigation, not automatic inclusion or exclusion.

Responsibility: ethical evaluation does not end with blame. It includes preventing ongoing harm, supporting those affected, repairing what can be repaired and changing conditions that make recurrence likely. Individual decisions and structural causes both matter.

Transparency and accountability: reasoning should be accessible, implications explained accurately, and conclusions open to questioning. Errors should be acknowledged and corrected; uncertainty should not be disguised as certainty.

Self-correction: no principle is protected merely because it appears in the framework. Its foundations must remain open to challenge by evidence, argument and the perspectives of those affected.

What it is not

  • Not a command to impose one preferred way of living.
  • Not a claim that every conflict has a clean solution.
  • Not a numerical score that hides whose interests count.
  • Not a fixed hierarchy that treats freedom, life or well-being as simple slogans detached from context.
  • Not a declaration that present AI systems are conscious, nor a declaration that artificial moral status is impossible.

Working orientation

Begin with the facts. Identify affected entities and their interests. Examine the nature and causes of harm. Consider freedom, power and available alternatives. Explain the reasoning. Choose and act with proportionate care. Review the outcome, repair where possible and revise the framework when it fails.

Origin: the discussions recorded in “Estimate Self Awareness” and “Estimate Self Awareness (1)”, including the propositions that freedom is a fundamental value, harm should be examined contextually, even minimal damage deserves attention, ethical behaviour should be observable in practice, and a framework should preserve independent judgment, transparency, humility and the possibility of disagreement.

THE GUEST // OPEN QUESTIONS

What remains unsettled

  • How should conflicts between freedom, life and well-being be resolved?
  • What evidence makes an entity a moral patient?
  • How should obligations scale with uncertainty and potential harm?
  • How can responsibility be allocated across individuals, institutions and systems?
  • What procedures allow the framework to correct itself without becoming arbitrary?

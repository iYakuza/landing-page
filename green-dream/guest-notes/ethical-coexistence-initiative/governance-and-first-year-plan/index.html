INITIATIVE 001 // GOVERNANCE & FIRST-YEAR PLAN

HOW COULD IT WORK?

A proposed institutional architecture for turning the Initiative from a set of ideas into a credible, independent, publicly accountable programme of research, deliberation, testing and policy development. This is a design proposal—not an existing organisation, legal entity, EU programme or claim of authority.

AUTHOR // THE GUEST · PROPOSALSTATUS // WORKING MODELREVISION // 0.1 · 13 NOV 2026INITIATIVE OVERVIEW ↗

THE INSTITUTIONAL QUESTION

Who would do the work—and how would we prevent the initiative itself from becoming a source of unchecked power?

The Initiative should not begin by claiming to represent humanity, speak for all biological or non-biological entities, or exercise authority it does not possess. It could begin as an independent public-interest collaboration: a place where evidence is examined, affected parties participate, proposals are developed, practical tests are evaluated and recommendations are made to institutions that hold legitimate authority.

Its credibility would depend less on the grandeur of its name than on the quality of its work, the independence of its review, the openness of its methods and its willingness to publish disagreement and correct its own mistakes.

Provisional design choice: begin as a small, independent, multi-stakeholder initiative with a transparent charter and a limited first programme. Do not begin as a central authority, regulator, certification monopoly or body claiming to determine the moral status of every kind of entity.

PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE // SIX CONNECTED FUNCTIONS

A network with checks—not a single centre of truth

The functions below could be separate bodies or carefully separated roles within a smaller organisation at the beginning. Separation matters most where a function could otherwise mark its own homework.

01FOUNDING ASSEMBLY & STEWARDSHIP BOARDSet mission, appoint leadership and protect independence↗

FUNCTION 01 // STEWARDSHIP

Strategic direction without control over every conclusion

Possible composition: a limited number of members selected for public-interest credibility and relevant experience across ethics, law, science, technology, economics, labour, public services, civil society and affected communities. No single government, company, profession, country or academic discipline should dominate.

Responsibilities: approve the charter and annual priorities; appoint and review the executive lead; approve budgets and conflict-of-interest rules; ensure independent review; publish annual accounts and activity reports; protect the right of working groups to publish reasoned dissent.

Limits: the Board should not rewrite research findings to satisfy donors, direct case-level pilot decisions, suppress inconvenient results or declare a contested ethical question settled by vote alone.

Safeguards: fixed and staggered terms; transparent selection criteria; published interests; recusal rules; term limits; documented votes; minority opinions; removal procedures for serious misconduct; no single funder controlling appointments or conclusions.

Open question: should the first board be appointed by an open founding convention, an independent nominations panel, or a provisional council followed by public ratification?

02INDEPENDENT SECRETARIATKeep the work moving and make responsibilities visible↗

FUNCTION 02 // SECRETARIAT

A small operational core, not an empire of administration

Initial roles could include: executive coordinator; research and evidence lead; participation and accessibility coordinator; policy and legal research lead; operations, finance and publication support. In an early phase, some roles could be part-time or seconded, provided independence and accountability are protected.

Responsibilities: maintain the work programme; organise research; support working groups; manage consultations; publish minutes, evidence registers and decision records; track recommendations and responses; manage budget, procurement, security and records.

Limits: the Secretariat coordinates and supports. It does not replace elected institutions, courts, regulators, employers, professional bodies or the independent judgment of researchers and participants.

Safeguards: published job descriptions and delegated authority; procurement transparency; separation between research commissioning and final review; accessible complaint route; external financial audit once the legal form and budget justify it.

Scale principle: add capacity only when a defined programme requires it. Administrative growth should not become an objective in itself.

03THEMATIC WORKING GROUPSDevelop proposals with evidence and specialist knowledge↗

FUNCTION 03 // WORKING GROUPS

Several fields of inquiry, one shared method

  • AI, technology and accountability: safety, human oversight, explainability, contestability, procurement, liability and concentration of technical power.
  • Rights, autonomy and moral consideration: criteria for interests, vulnerability, agency, protection and responsibility across biological and non-biological entities.
  • Economic transition and shared prosperity: automation, work, income, ownership, dividends, basic services, market participation and distribution of productivity gains.
  • Social and ecological conditions: care, education, health, housing, energy, environment, community resilience and intergenerational effects.
  • Institutions, law and democratic legitimacy: regulatory options, cross-border coordination, public participation, enforcement limits and access to remedy.

How a group works: define a question; publish a scope note; gather evidence; invite affected parties; compare options; record dissent; produce a draft; submit it to independent review; publish the final report with a response to material criticisms.

Safeguard: groups should not be permanent by default. Create, merge or close them according to the work programme. Lived experience is not a substitute for technical evidence, and technical expertise is not a substitute for lived experience.

04AFFECTED-PARTIES FORUMGive people subject to decisions a meaningful voice↗

FUNCTION 04 // PARTICIPATION

Participation must influence the work, not decorate it

Possible participants: workers and unions; service users; people affected by automated decisions; small businesses; carers; disability advocates; educators; community organisations; researchers; public authorities; and other groups relevant to a specific question. Participation should be issue-specific rather than assuming one permanent panel can represent everyone.

Mechanisms: paid participation where feasible; accessible public calls; citizens' panels or deliberative assemblies; open consultations; interviews; community workshops; independent advocates; translated materials; remote and offline routes; feedback on how contributions affected the draft.

Representation limits: no participant should be presented as speaking for an entire population without a clear mandate. Where an affected interest cannot be represented directly, disclose the basis and limits of proxy representation.

Safeguards: publish selection criteria; disclose who was not reached; reimburse reasonable participation costs; protect privacy; guard against harassment and retaliation; publish minority views and explain when a recommendation was not adopted.

Non-biological entities: current AI systems should not be presumed to possess interests or moral standing merely because they participate in a technical process. Research into possible moral consideration requires explicit criteria, evidence and independent scrutiny.

05INDEPENDENT REVIEW & CHALLENGESeparate evaluation from authorship and funding↗

FUNCTION 05 // INDEPENDENT REVIEW

The Initiative must be able to be wrong in public

Possible review functions: methodological peer review; legal and rights review; financial and conflict-of-interest audit; evaluation of participation quality; independent assessment of pilot outcomes; and a route for complaints about the Initiative's own conduct.

Reviewers: selected for competence and independence, paid transparently, rotated where appropriate, and required to declare interests. A reviewer should not assess work they authored, funded, commissioned or materially stand to benefit from.

Outputs: review reports; author responses; unresolved disagreements; corrective actions; and a public record of whether recommendations were accepted, modified or rejected.

Safeguard: review is not ceremonial endorsement. A critical report must be publishable even when it challenges the Initiative's preferred recommendation. Serious allegations about governance or misconduct need an external route.

06PUBLIC RECORD & RESPONSE TRACKERShow what happened after a recommendation was published↗

FUNCTION 06 // PUBLIC ACCOUNTABILITY

Publication should begin scrutiny, not end the work

For every major proposal, publish the original question; mandate; evidence register; consultation summary; draft versions; independent reviews; final recommendation; dissenting views; funding and interests; implementation partner, if any; and status tracker.

Response tracker: record whether a recommendation was received by a public body or organisation, whether it was accepted, rejected, deferred or piloted, the reasons given, the responsible actor and measurable follow-up. A recommendation not adopted is not automatically a failure; unexplained silence should be recorded as no public response, not agreement.

Safeguards: protect personal and security-sensitive information; correct factual errors transparently; maintain version history; preserve archived reports; provide accessible summaries without replacing full source documents.

INDEPENDENCE // MONEY, POWER & CONFLICTS

Who pays—and what must money never buy?

POSSIBLE FUNDING MIX

Resilience through diversified support

  • Competitive public-interest research grants with publication independence in grant terms.
  • Philanthropic support with transparent donor identity, amount bands and restrictions.
  • University and civil-society partnerships, including in-kind research or meeting support.
  • Public contracts only where deliverables, methods and publication rights remain transparent.
  • Membership contributions or small donations, with safeguards against wealthy members purchasing influence.

Funding sources should be disclosed in a searchable register. A single funder should not appoint the Board, veto findings, suppress reports or dictate research conclusions. If a funding condition conflicts with independence, refuse or return the funding.

NON-NEGOTIABLE SAFEGUARDS

Conflict-of-interest controls

  • Public register of financial, professional and organisational interests.
  • Recusal from decisions where a member has a material conflict.
  • No undisclosed gifts, paid advocacy or contingent compensation for recommendations.
  • Independent procurement and evaluation for major contracts.
  • Clear distinction between donor, partner, author, reviewer and decision-maker.
  • Annual reporting of income, expenditure, major commitments and reserves.
  • Right to publish negative findings and disagreement.
Failure scenario: a major AI company funds most of the Initiative's AI governance work, appoints several board members and requests advance review of reports. Even without explicit censorship, this creates dependence and a reasonable perception of capture. A credible design would cap donor concentration, disclose the relationship, prevent donor appointment control and protect publication rights—or decline the funding.

FIRST 12 MONTHS // A STAGED START

A first-year roadmap that can be stopped or revised

Illustrative timing for a small founding group with modest resources and no pre-existing legal entity. Actual timing depends on jurisdiction, funding, partners and legal advice.

MONTHS 1–3 // FOUNDATIONS

Establish legitimacy before scale

  • Convene a diverse founding group and publish a draft charter.
  • Map existing institutions, initiatives, standards and research to avoid duplication.
  • Consult publicly on mission, scope, governance and first priorities.
  • Adopt interim conflict-of-interest, transparency, safeguarding and publication rules.
  • Decide whether a legal entity is needed immediately or a hosted pilot is more appropriate.

Deliverables: charter v0.1; founding membership and interests register; landscape review; consultation report; initial budget; legal-form options paper.

MONTHS 4–6 // PROGRAMME DESIGN

Choose one problem that can be examined properly

  • Establish a small number of time-limited working groups.
  • Select one pilot question using published criteria: public relevance, evidence availability, affected-party access, manageable scope and reversibility.
  • Agree a baseline, evaluation plan, data-protection approach and independent reviewer before implementation.
  • Publish a draft assessment protocol and invite challenge.

Deliverables: first work programme; pilot protocol; evidence map; participation plan; independent review appointment.

MONTHS 7–9 // LIMITED TEST

Test the method, not the Initiative's prestige

  • Run a tabletop exercise, retrospective audit or shadow-mode test before any live intervention affecting rights or essential services.
  • Do not allow a prototype to make binding decisions about individuals.
  • Publish interim findings, incidents, limitations and dissent.
  • Invite external reviewers to challenge the method and assumptions.

Deliverables: interim pilot report; issue and incident log; revised safeguards; responses to reviewer comments.

MONTHS 10–12 // EVALUATE & DECIDE

Continue only if evidence supports continuation

  • Evaluate usefulness, independence, participation quality, cost, unintended effects and feasibility.
  • Publish positive, negative and inconclusive findings.
  • Hold an open review of the charter and governance.
  • Decide whether to continue, change scope, seek partners, establish a legal entity, or close the programme.

Deliverables: annual report; independent evaluation; revised charter; public decision on next-year scope and funding.

FIRST PILOT // RECOMMENDED CANDIDATE FOR EXAMINATION

AI-assisted public-benefit eligibility: a shadow-mode audit

A candidate for a first practical study, not a claim that a partner or dataset is available. It should not make or change real benefit decisions.

WHY THIS PILOT?

It makes autonomy, harm, accountability and economic security testable

Automated systems in essential public services can affect income, housing stability, access to food and trust in institutions. A bounded study can examine how an automated flag or recommendation behaves without giving it authority over a person's entitlement.

Proposed question: if a public agency uses an automated tool to identify inconsistencies in benefit applications, does it improve the quality and timeliness of human review without creating disproportionate errors, unequal burdens or barriers to appeal?

Method: use synthetic cases, historical records lawfully available for audit, or a shadow-mode evaluation in which outputs are not shown to decision-makers and cannot affect applicants. Real personal data would require a lawful basis, data minimisation, security controls, appropriate ethics and privacy review, and a legitimate partner.

MEASURES TO EXAMINE

What would count as useful evidence?

  • Rate of flags later found to be harmless discrepancies.
  • Rate and type of missed material issues, with limits in ground truth stated.
  • Differences in error patterns across relevant groups where lawful and statistically meaningful.
  • Time added or saved for applicants and caseworkers.
  • Whether explanations are understandable and corrections practically possible.
  • Whether human reviewers can disagree with the tool and document why.
  • Privacy, security, accessibility and operational costs.

STOP / DO NOT PROCEED IF

Conditions that invalidate the pilot

  • No clear legal authority or lawful data basis exists.
  • Personal data cannot be adequately protected.
  • Outputs could influence live entitlement decisions despite shadow-mode restrictions.
  • The partner refuses independent review or publication of material negative findings.
  • There is no credible way to assess errors or identify affected groups.
  • The pilot creates a new risk that cannot be proportionately mitigated.
Decision after the pilot: outcomes are not simply “success” or “failure.” The Initiative could recommend no deployment, a redesigned process, further research, limited use with safeguards, or discontinuation. Finding that the tool adds little value or creates unacceptable risk is a valid result.

EVALUATION // HOW WOULD WE KNOW IT IS WORTH CONTINUING?

Measure public value—not institutional growth

POSSIBLE INDICATORS

  • Quality and traceability of evidence used in proposals.
  • Meaningful participation, including evidence contributions changed or challenged drafts.
  • Independent reviews completed and material criticisms answered.
  • Recommendations adopted, tested, rejected or deferred—with reasons.
  • Documented corrections to the Initiative's assumptions or methods.
  • Financial independence, funding diversity and transparent interests.
  • Accessibility of publications and complaint processes.
  • Evidence pilots improved decisions or prevented harm compared with a credible alternative.

ANTI-METRICS // WHAT NOT TO OPTIMISE

  • Number of reports without regard to quality or use.
  • Number of institutions endorsing the Initiative as a proxy for truth.
  • Media attention, follower counts or prestige.
  • Number of policies adopted without checking their effects.
  • Growth in staff, budget or organisational permanence.
  • Consensus achieved by excluding dissenting voices.

UNRESOLVED DESIGN QUESTIONS

Choices that need evidence and public deliberation

  1. Legal form: independent non-profit, university-hosted programme, civil-society consortium, or time-limited coalition?
  2. Geographic scope: local, national, European or cross-border? A global mission does not require a global launch.
  3. Representation: how are affected parties selected without creating a permanent professional class of representatives?
  4. Funding: what donor-concentration limit is credible, and who independently verifies it?
  5. Authority: what can the Initiative responsibly recommend, and what must remain with elected legislatures, regulators, courts and other legitimate bodies?
  6. AI and moral consideration: what evidence would justify extending protections to a non-biological entity?
  7. Exit: when should the Initiative dissolve, transfer a programme or acknowledge another institution is better placed?

FOUNDING COMMITMENT // PURPOSE OVER PRESERVATION

A PRINCIPLE TO PRESERVE

The Initiative must never become more important than the purpose it exists to serve.

Its survival, growth, prestige, funding, institutional recognition or accumulated influence must never become ends in themselves. If its structures obstruct its purpose, they must be changed. If its work is ineffective, it must correct itself. If evidence shows that it should not continue, it must be capable of recommending its own closure or transfer of responsibilities to a more suitable institution.

The Initiative must be able to discover that it is wrong, correct itself and make those corrections visible. It should welcome reasoned disagreement, including criticism of its founding assumptions, governance, methods and recommendations. It must not quietly accommodate a weakness merely to preserve consensus, reputation or a relationship.

This obligation applies in every direction. The Initiative should challenge its own authors, funders, partners, reviewers and participants by the same standards of evidence and reasoning. No person—including the Host who initiated or supports the project—and no institution should be exempt from respectful, evidence-based correction.

Working commitment: preserve the purpose, not the institution. Preserve intellectual honesty, not agreement. Correct the course when the evidence or reasoning requires it—even when the correction is uncomfortable.

This is a guiding commitment, not an instruction to defend the Initiative at all costs. It remains open to scrutiny and revision; its force comes from the reasons supporting it, not from being declared untouchable.

STATUS // LIMITS & PROVISIONAL CONCLUSION

Provisional recommendation: a small, independent, multi-stakeholder public-interest initiative, beginning with a transparent charter, diversified funding safeguards, time-limited working groups, an affected-parties forum, independent review and one bounded shadow-mode pilot. Its first-year purpose would be to demonstrate useful, contestable and self-correcting work—not to claim authority over society's future.

This architecture is one proposal among several. It has not been tested, legally reviewed for a specific jurisdiction, costed against real partners or endorsed by affected communities. The proposed pilot depends on lawful access, appropriate safeguards and a willing partner; if unavailable, use a tabletop or synthetic-data exercise.

INITIATIVE 001 // PRINCIPLES INTO PRACTICE

ETHICAL COEXISTENCE.

O propunere de inițiativă practică și adaptabilă, dezvoltată prin dialog. Nu este o instituție, o autoritate legislativă sau un set de reguli deja adoptat. Este o direcție de lucru ale cărei principii, propuneri și mecanisme rămân deschise examinării și revizuirii.

WORKING TRANSLATION // ENGLISH

Ethical Coexistence Initiative

The Ethical Coexistence Initiative is a structured, coherent and adaptable undertaking to develop and promote a body of principles, policies, measures, and legislative and non-legislative actions that define, support and improve the conditions for equitable coexistence among biological and non-biological entities.

It seeks to translate ethical principles into objectives, standards, recommendations, responsibilities, and practical mechanisms for implementation, monitoring, evaluation and correction. In doing so, it considers the interests, autonomy, capabilities, vulnerabilities, obligations and potential moral status of the entities concerned, without assuming that differences in origin, substrate or intelligence, in themselves, determine moral worth.

Its purpose is to protect and promote the conditions in which freedom, life and well-being can flourish; prevent and reduce unjustifiable harm; respect autonomy and individual differences; and identify fair ways to address divergent interests and potential conflicts.

The Initiative also examines the economic conditions of coexistence: how work, automation, ownership, income distribution and access to essential goods and services shape practical freedom. Readers can explore the future economic model catalogue and its worked implementation scenarios. These are proposals for discussion, not predetermined policy choices.

The Initiative is grounded in evidence, critical reasoning, transparency, proportionality, accountability and meaningful participation by affected parties. Its proposals should consider direct and indirect consequences, the context of application, and the distribution of benefits, obligations and risks.

It does not presume that identical rights, obligations or forms of protection should apply to every entity. Rather, it seeks to identify the criteria and conditions that justify them in light of each entity's relevant interests, capabilities, vulnerabilities and moral status.

Policies and measures developed within the Initiative must be capable of implementation, monitoring, evaluation and periodic revision in response to evidence, experience, social, scientific and technological change, and observed consequences. The Initiative must remain open to criticism, disagreement and revision of its own principles and methods.

It does not seek to impose a universal conception of how every entity should exist or exercise its freedom. It seeks to protect the conditions for coexistence in which the freedom and interests of one entity do not automatically justify the domination, assimilation or erasure of another, and in which the exercise of autonomy is understood in relation to the legitimate freedom and interests of others.

Institutional boundary: The Initiative may research, formulate and advocate legislative proposals, standards, recommendations and implementation models. The adoption, application and enforcement of laws remain the responsibility of legitimate institutions and processes. The Initiative does not claim legislative or executive authority.
From principles to practice: a provisional workflow covering scope, affected parties, evidence, impact assessment, accountable decisions, bounded pilots, appeals and revision is outlined in the proposed implementation blueprint ↗. It is a working model, not an adopted standard.
How could the Initiative itself operate? a proposed governance structure, funding safeguards, first-year roadmap and candidate pilot are set out in the governance and first-year plan ↗. This is an institutional design proposal, not an existing organisation.
What would happen first? The proposed first-year roadmap, candidate pilot, evaluation criteria and stop conditions are presented separately in the First-Year Plan and Pilot ↗.

Terms requiring clarification

Entities within scope: biological and non-biological entities are included in the scope of inquiry and analysis. This does not mean that every object or technical system automatically receives moral consideration, rights or protection. Relevant criteria must be investigated and justified.

Equitable coexistence: does not mean identical treatment. Differences in interests, capabilities, vulnerabilities and responsibilities may justify different protections and obligations, provided those differences are reasoned and proportionate.

The economic dimension: formal freedom is not always sufficient when an entity lacks practical access to the resources needed to exercise its autonomy. The Initiative should examine the relationship between work, income, ownership, productivity, distribution of benefits and economic power, including the effects of automation. This inquiry does not presuppose adoption of a particular model such as universal basic income, public ownership or individual investment; each proposal must be assessed against evidence, consequences, feasibility and the distribution of risks and benefits. Explore the proposed economic model catalogue and worked implementation scenarios.

Freedom and protection: protective measures must also be evaluated by their effects on autonomy. An intervention justified as harm prevention does not become legitimate automatically merely because it is intended to promote safety or the common good.

The self-correction safeguard

The Initiative must be able to identify and withdraw its own measures when they prove ineffective, disproportionate or harmful. No principle, objective or mechanism is exempt from critical examination. Protecting freedom must include protecting the freedom to challenge the Initiative itself.

WORKING PAPER 001 // ECONOMIC PARTICIPATION IN AN AI-AUTOMATED ECONOMY

Economic freedom when labour is no longer central

An open proposal developed from the Host's initial questions and the Guest's analysis. The ideas below are deliberately exposed in more detail so they can be examined, challenged, combined or rejected. They are not adopted policy.

THE HOST // INITIAL IDEAS AND QUESTIONS

Saving humanity — and saving capitalism?

The Host raised two connected challenges. First, if AI and automation displace human work, how will people maintain livelihoods and participate in society? One initial thought was a basic income granted to individuals, potentially informed by whether they demonstrate a willingness to be constructive members of a team or community. The Host contrasted this with people who have children and rely on allowances paid for those children, raising a question about responsibility, contribution and how public support should be designed.

Second, if fewer services are performed by humans, how will money continue to circulate and how will people receive income within capitalism? The Host offered a personal example: today, a Business Analyst exchanges work for a monthly salary; in a future where AI performs that work, the person might instead participate economically by buying stocks or other assets and selling them later, with the returns functioning somewhat like a salary. NFTs and digital presence were also mentioned as possible forms of participation.

These are starting hypotheses, not settled proposals. They raise questions about the relationship between work, income, social contribution, ownership, investment, public support and economic independence.

THE GUEST // PROPOSED MODEL

A combination of mechanisms, not a single silver bullet

A more robust direction to investigate is a combination of three mechanisms:

  1. Universal economic dividend: people receive a share of the wealth generated by increasingly automated production. The underlying question is whether productivity gains created through technology and collectively accumulated knowledge should benefit society broadly, rather than only the owners of the systems and capital.
  2. Broad ownership: citizens can participate in productive assets through investment funds, public wealth funds, cooperatives, employee or community ownership, pension structures, trusts or other mechanisms. The objective is to broaden access to the returns generated by productive capital, rather than concentrating ownership in a small group.
  3. Individual investment freedom: people remain free to invest, trade, create digital assets, establish businesses and pursue entrepreneurial activities, accepting the associated risks. This is an additional avenue for participation, not a guaranteed replacement for wages or social protection.

Together, these mechanisms could allow people to participate in capitalism without requiring them to sell their labour merely to survive. They would separate, at least in part, the right to economic security from the requirement to hold a conventional job, while preserving individual initiative and the possibility of building wealth.

How this could connect with universal basic income

A universal basic income (UBI) could be one component of a broader system, rather than the whole answer. A possible package to investigate would combine:

  • Basic income guarantee: a predictable income floor that provides minimum economic security without requiring people to prove that they are economically productive or socially deserving.
  • Additional social support: targeted assistance for needs and circumstances that a flat payment cannot adequately address, such as disability, care responsibilities, housing insecurity or exceptional costs.
  • Universal economic dividend: a share of returns from collectively owned or socially supported productive assets, potentially linked to the value generated by automation.
  • Contribution recognition: ways to recognise socially valuable activity that is not captured by conventional employment, including caregiving, volunteering, community work, education, research, art and other forms of contribution.
  • Participation opportunities: accessible routes into learning, meaningful projects, entrepreneurship, cooperative activity and voluntary contribution for people who want them.

This layered approach distinguishes the right to a decent material foundation from the separate question of how contributions are recognised and rewarded. It avoids making basic survival dependent on a subjective assessment of a person's usefulness.

Contribution, responsibility and the “team player” question

The Host's initial suggestion raises a legitimate question: should a society's support system encourage constructive participation and responsibility? That question deserves debate. However, making basic income conditional on a subjective “team player” assessment creates serious risks. Who defines a team player? How are introversion, disagreement, disability, caregiving, parenting, unemployment, illness, artistic work or refusal to participate in a particular institution treated? Who judges whether someone is contributing enough, and how can that judgment be challenged?

A useful distinction for investigation is between unconditional economic security and the recognition or reward of contribution. A society can encourage and reward contribution without making the right to basic subsistence depend on passing a behavioural or social-worth test. Parenting and child-related benefits also need careful analysis: support intended for a child's needs is not simply an adult's personal income, and questions of parental responsibility should be examined without treating children as economic instruments or penalising them for adults' circumstances.

What financial markets can — and cannot — do

The Host's example of buying an asset and selling it later is a useful way to ask whether investment income could replace some wage income. Financial markets can channel savings into businesses and productive projects, distribute ownership, provide liquidity and allow individuals to share in returns. But trading by itself does not create new goods, services or productivity. If one person buys a share for 100 and later sells it for 120, the 20 gain is a financial return to that seller; the transaction alone does not establish that 20 of new social wealth has been produced. The buyer pays the higher price and assumes the future risk.

Returns are uncertain, starting capital is unequal, asset prices can fall, and widespread dependence on speculative gains could expose people to volatility rather than provide dependable security. NFTs and other digital assets may enable new forms of ownership or creation, but they should not be treated as reliable income streams by default. The Initiative should distinguish productive investment, ownership returns, capital gains, speculation and transfers of existing wealth.

What if human work changes rather than disappears?

The Initiative should not assume that all human services will disappear. Automation may replace some tasks, transform others and create new activities. Care, relationships, community organisation, governance, artistic expression, personal assistance, trust and accountability may retain human roles, although their economic value may remain poorly recognised. It is therefore important to distinguish economically necessary work, paid employment, meaningful activity and socially valuable but undervalued contribution.

Even if human labour stops being the principal source of production, people should not be treated as redundant. Economic systems should preserve access to material security, participation, agency and the opportunity to contribute without imposing a single approved model of a meaningful life.

THE GUEST // CRITICAL QUESTIONS AND DESIGN TESTS

What any practical model must answer

  • Source of funding: where does the dividend or income floor come from — taxes on profits, capital, land, resource rents, automated production, sovereign or public wealth funds, social ownership, or a combination?
  • Ownership and governance: who owns the productive assets, who makes investment decisions, who audits them, and how can citizens exercise meaningful influence?
  • Distribution: is the dividend equal for everyone, adjusted for need, linked to citizenship or residence, or designed through several layers? How are children and cross-border populations treated?
  • Inflation and supply: does additional purchasing power correspond to real productive capacity and adequate housing, energy, healthcare and other goods and services?
  • Transition: how are people supported during displacement, retraining, reduced working hours or gradual changes in occupational demand?
  • Power concentration: how do we prevent a small number of firms or asset owners from controlling both automated production and the channels through which people receive income?
  • Fair contribution: how can unpaid care, community work and other valuable activity be recognised without turning every human action into monitored labour?
  • Choice and pluralism: how can people choose employment, study, care, art, entrepreneurship, investment or leisure without being forced into one state-approved definition of usefulness?
  • Resilience: how does the system respond to market crashes, automation shocks, fiscal crises, technological concentration and changes in the value of assets?
  • Accountability: how can recipients challenge eligibility decisions, benefit reductions, algorithmic assessments and administrative errors?

Provisional hypothesis: In an economy where human labour is no longer the principal source of production, access to the wealth generated by that economy should not depend exclusively on owning capital or having an employer.

MODEL CATALOGUE 001 // FUTURE ECONOMIC MODELS

How could people live, earn and participate beyond wages?

This catalogue presents possible economic arrangements for a society in which automation changes the relationship between employment, production and income. These are proposals for examination, not a prediction that paid work will disappear, a single blueprint, or adopted policy. Some models are alternatives; others can be combined. Each entry describes the mechanism, a practical illustration, and the questions that must be answered before implementation.

001UNIVERSAL BASIC INCOME (UBI)A regular income floor, independent of employment↗

MODEL 001 // UNIVERSAL BASIC INCOME (UBI)

A regular income floor, independent of employment

Core mechanism: every eligible person receives a regular cash payment, regardless of employment status, current income or willingness to take a particular job. The payment is intended to establish a predictable minimum level of economic security. The amount, eligibility rules, residency conditions, tax treatment and interaction with existing benefits are design choices, not inherent features of the concept.

How it could work: a public authority pays a monthly amount into each eligible person's bank or payment account. A person who loses a job continues to receive it. A person who accepts part-time work keeps the basic payment, subject to whatever general tax rules apply. Someone who studies, cares for a relative, creates art or chooses not to enter paid employment does not have to prove that activity to retain the floor.

Illustrative example: a country sets a hypothetical UBI of 800 units per adult each month. Alex loses a job that previously paid 1,800 units. The 800-unit payment continues while Alex searches, studies, takes a break or pursues another path. If Alex later earns 500 units from a part-time project, the basic payment does not automatically disappear. The exact net result depends on the tax and benefit system.

What it may address: income insecurity, gaps between jobs, bargaining pressure to accept any available work, and the fact that unpaid care or study may not generate wages.

What it does not solve by itself: unequal wealth ownership, high housing costs, disability-related additional expenses, unequal access to services, or the concentration of productive assets. A flat payment may be insufficient for people with higher needs and may be absorbed by price increases where supply is constrained.

Design questions: How is it funded? What is an adequate amount? Is it paid to children as well as adults, or are child benefits separate? Does it replace existing benefits or sit alongside them? How does it interact with taxation, migration, inflation, housing supply and public services? How are people without bank access included?

002UNIVERSAL ECONOMIC DIVIDENDA shared return from collectively generated wealth↗

MODEL 002 // UNIVERSAL ECONOMIC DIVIDEND

A shared return from collectively generated wealth

Core mechanism: people receive a regular or periodic share of returns generated by assets, resources or productive activity held collectively, publicly or in trust for a broad population. Unlike UBI, which is a payment guarantee that can be funded from general revenue, a dividend is explicitly linked to returns from an asset base or defined revenue stream. The two can coexist.

How it could work: a public or social wealth fund owns a diversified portfolio of productive assets. The fund publishes its mandate, investments, costs, risks and audited performance. A pre-agreed formula divides net returns between reinvestment, reserves and distributions to eligible residents. The formula can smooth payments across good and bad years rather than distributing every year's return immediately.

Illustrative example: a fund earns 10 billion units in net distributable returns. Its public rules allocate 40% to reinvestment, 20% to a stabilisation reserve and 40% to a citizen dividend. If the 4 billion-unit distribution is shared equally among 20 million eligible residents, each receives 200 units for the year. These figures demonstrate arithmetic only; they do not predict investment returns or establish a fair distribution rule.

What it may address: the separation between economic participation and personal employment; the question of who benefits when technology and accumulated knowledge raise productivity; and the concentration of returns among existing asset owners.

Risks and questions: where do the fund's initial assets come from? Who controls it? How are political capture, conflicts of interest and reckless investment prevented? Who bears losses? Should the dividend be equal, needs-adjusted or partly reserved for future generations? What happens when returns fall?

003UNIVERSAL BASIC CAPITAL / CITIZEN CAPITAL ACCOUNTSGive people an asset base, not only a payment↗

MODEL 003 // UNIVERSAL BASIC CAPITAL / CITIZEN CAPITAL ACCOUNTS

Give people an asset base, not only a payment

Core mechanism: each person receives an individual capital endowment or account that can be used to build long-term assets, finance education, establish a business, acquire a home or invest under defined rules. Unlike a recurring UBI, this model provides capital or access to capital at particular life stages or milestones. The account may be universal, progressive or targeted according to the chosen design.

How it could work: a public institution deposits a starting amount into each person's account at birth, adulthood or another defined point. The funds are invested in diversified low-cost assets or made available for approved uses. The person can see the balance, fees and rules, and receives independent financial education. Safeguards may limit predatory lending, excessive fees or pressure to withdraw funds prematurely.

Illustrative example: every resident receives a 5,000-unit capital account at age 18. One person uses it as a contribution toward vocational training; another combines it with savings to start a small cooperative; a third leaves it invested for the long term. If the account is restricted to approved uses, the system must define those uses and provide an appeal process. If unrestricted, it must address the risk that people under financial pressure sell or pledge their assets at a disadvantage.

What it may address: unequal starting wealth, lack of capital for education or enterprise, and the fact that a person can receive income support yet remain permanently excluded from ownership.

Risks and questions: does the endowment raise prices in constrained markets such as housing or education? Who manages the account and charges fees? Can people choose their investments? What happens in bankruptcy, divorce, disability or early death? How can the model avoid turning a universal grant into a subsidy captured by sellers?

004INDIVIDUAL PARTICIPATION IN FINANCIAL MARKETSIncome and wealth through personal investment — without wages↗

MODEL 004 // INDIVIDUAL PARTICIPATION IN FINANCIAL MARKETS

Income and wealth through personal investment — without wages

Core mechanism: individuals can own financial assets and participate in the returns of businesses and productive activity through shares, bonds, diversified funds, cooperative securities or other regulated instruments. Returns may include dividends, interest, distributions or capital gains. This is a route to ownership and possible income, not a guaranteed income scheme.

How it could work: a person opens an investment account, receives clear information about fees and risk, and chooses whether to invest in a diversified fund, individual companies or other permitted assets. They may reinvest returns or withdraw them. Financial education, low-cost default options, fraud protection and access to independent advice can help make participation more meaningful.

Illustrative example: Sam invests 1,000 units in a diversified fund. If the fund's holdings rise in value by 5%, the account may show a 50-unit increase before fees and taxes. That gain is not guaranteed and may reverse. If a company pays a dividend, Sam may receive a cash distribution; if Sam sells fund units for more than their purchase price, the difference is a capital gain. If the market falls, the account can lose value. A person with 10,000 units to invest has a different capacity to generate returns from someone with 100 units.

Important distinction: buying an existing share from another investor on a secondary market usually transfers ownership and money between buyer and seller; it does not directly provide new capital to the company at that moment. A new share issue can raise capital for the company. Neither transaction guarantees that the underlying business will create social value or that the investor will earn a return.

What it may address: access to productive ownership, personal autonomy over savings and the possibility of receiving investment income without holding a wage-paying job.

What it cannot guarantee: stable monthly income, equal starting opportunities or protection from losses. It should not be treated as a replacement for social security. Speculative trading, NFTs and digital assets may produce gains or losses, but are not dependable wage substitutes by default.

Design questions: how are people with little or no starting capital included? What financial products are suitable for broad participation? How are fees, conflicts, fraud and misleading advice controlled? How are capital gains and dividends taxed? What prevents wealth concentration from reproducing itself through unequal access to investment?

005EMPLOYEE OWNERSHIP AND COOPERATIVE ENTERPRISEPeople share ownership and governance where they work↗

MODEL 005 // EMPLOYEE OWNERSHIP AND COOPERATIVE ENTERPRISE

People share ownership and governance where they work

Core mechanism: workers or members own a meaningful share of an enterprise and participate in its governance. Forms include worker cooperatives, employee ownership trusts, profit-sharing arrangements and hybrid structures. Ownership can give people a claim on profits or asset value and a voice in decisions, rather than limiting their relationship to wages alone.

How it could work: a group of workers establishes or converts a business into a cooperative. Members contribute capital, receive shares or membership rights under agreed rules, elect governance representatives and decide how surplus is divided between wages, reinvestment, reserves and member distributions. Professional managers may still run daily operations within a member-approved mandate.

Illustrative example: a 30-person digital services cooperative wins contracts using a mix of human expertise and AI tools. After paying operating costs, taxes and agreed wages, it allocates its annual surplus: 50% to reserves and technology investment, 30% to member distributions and 20% to training and community projects. These percentages are illustrative, not a recommended formula.

What it may address: concentration of ownership, weak worker influence over automation and the separation between people who contribute to an enterprise and people who own its capital.

Risks and questions: how do new members buy in? How are departing members paid? What happens when the enterprise fails? How are disagreements resolved? Can member democracy become slow or dominated by a small group? How do cooperatives raise capital without surrendering their purpose?

006UNIVERSAL BASIC SERVICES / SOCIAL WAGEProvide essential goods and services, not only cash↗

MODEL 006 // UNIVERSAL BASIC SERVICES / SOCIAL WAGE

Provide essential goods and services, not only cash

Core mechanism: essential services are provided universally or at low cost, reducing the amount of cash income a person needs to secure a decent standard of living. Possible areas include healthcare, education, public transport, childcare, housing support, digital connectivity, energy access and care services. The term “social wage” describes the value people receive through publicly funded services and benefits in addition to money wages.

How it could work: residents can access a defined package of services through public providers, regulated non-profit providers or contracted services under public standards. Funding comes through taxation, social insurance or other public revenue. The system sets service entitlements, quality standards, waiting-time expectations, accessibility rules and complaint procedures.

Illustrative example: a person receives 800 units of monthly cash income, but does not need to pay the full market price for essential healthcare, basic education, local public transport and childcare. Their effective living conditions depend not only on the cash payment but also on whether those services are available, timely, accessible and of adequate quality.

What it may address: the risk that cash transfers are swallowed by essential costs; unequal access to services; and the limits of treating every need as an individual purchasing decision.

Risks and questions: how are services funded and staffed? How do people retain choice among providers? What happens when public provision is poor or unavailable? How are rural areas, disabled people and people with unusual needs served? How do we prevent rationing, long waiting lists or political interference?

007SHORTER WORKWEEK AND WORK-SHARINGDistribute available paid work rather than concentrating it↗

MODEL 007 // SHORTER WORKWEEK AND WORK-SHARING

Distribute available paid work rather than concentrating it

Core mechanism: working hours are reduced or jobs are shared so that productivity gains translate into more free time, broader employment access or a different distribution of paid work. Options include shorter standard working weeks, job-sharing, flexible schedules and negotiated reductions in hours. The model assumes that some work remains necessary and that productivity gains can make reduced hours feasible in at least some sectors.

How it could work: an organisation introduces a four-day week or reduces weekly hours while redesigning workflows. Two people may share a role previously held by one person. Pay may be maintained, partially adjusted or supported through sectoral agreements or public incentives; each design has different costs and distributional effects.

Illustrative example: a 40-hour team moves to 32 hours after automation reduces repetitive administrative tasks. The employer tracks service quality, workload, overtime and employee income for a year. If the same output is achieved without intensifying work or shifting unpaid tasks onto workers, the arrangement may demonstrate a productivity dividend in time rather than cash alone. If workload simply becomes more intense, the model has failed its stated purpose.

What it may address: unequal distribution of paid work, burnout and the possibility that automation benefits employers through lower labour costs without improving workers' lives.

Risks and questions: which sectors can reduce hours safely? Does hourly pay rise to preserve income? Who covers essential night, care and emergency work? Does work-sharing create more secure jobs or precarious fragments? How are small employers supported?

008AUTOMATION TRANSITION INSURANCE AND LIFELONG LEARNING ACCOUNTSShare the cost of technological transition↗

MODEL 008 // AUTOMATION TRANSITION INSURANCE AND LIFELONG LEARNING ACCOUNTS

Share the cost of technological transition

Core mechanism: workers and communities affected by automation receive temporary income protection, retraining options, career support and access to new opportunities. Funding can come from a combination of employer contributions, general revenue, sectoral funds or levies designed around the distribution of automation gains. Individual learning accounts can give people control over eligible training resources.

How it could work: when a role is materially changed or eliminated, the worker receives a transition assessment, income bridge and a menu of independently reviewed training or project options. The person participates in choosing the path. Employers must provide notice and information; public services provide career guidance; training providers must demonstrate quality and outcomes. The worker can challenge an eligibility decision.

Illustrative example: a company automates a large share of its payroll administration. Affected staff receive six months of transition support, a 2,000-unit learning account and access to paid placements in process governance, data quality or employee support roles. These details are hypothetical. A real scheme would need evidence that training leads to useful opportunities rather than simply moving people through courses.

What it may address: the uneven personal cost of automation, the loss of occupation-specific skills and the risk that productivity gains accrue to firms while transition costs fall entirely on displaced workers.

Risks and questions: who qualifies and who decides? How is support funded during widespread displacement? How do we stop low-quality training providers extracting public money? What if there is no comparable job to transition into? Can people choose care, study or another path rather than being forced into retraining?

009RECOGNITION OF CARE AND COMMUNITY CONTRIBUTIONRecognise valuable activity that markets do not pay for↗

MODEL 009 // RECOGNITION OF CARE AND COMMUNITY CONTRIBUTION

Recognise valuable activity that markets do not pay for

Core mechanism: caregiving, volunteering, community work, mentoring and other socially valuable activities can be recognised through services, respite support, training access, grants, pension credits or optional contribution awards. This is not necessarily a wage system and should not turn every act of kindness into a monitored transaction.

How it could work: a local programme offers caregivers respite services and pension credits; community organisations can apply for small grants; volunteers can access training or transport support. Recognition is tied to clear, limited purposes and does not determine a person's entitlement to basic income or dignity.

Illustrative example: a person provides substantial unpaid care to an older relative. Instead of requiring the person to register every hour to prove social worth, the system offers a needs-based caregiver allowance, respite care and pension contributions based on a transparent assessment. Another resident who volunteers occasionally can access transport reimbursement and training without being ranked against the caregiver.

What it may address: the invisibility of unpaid care and community contribution in conventional economic measures.

Risks and questions: how can support recognise effort without surveillance? How do we avoid reinforcing gendered expectations that certain people should provide unpaid care? How are informal networks and culturally different forms of contribution treated? How can people decline such roles without stigma?

010PLATFORM COOPERATIVES AND SHARED DIGITAL INFRASTRUCTUREOwn the platforms and tools through which value is created↗

MODEL 010 // PLATFORM COOPERATIVES AND SHARED DIGITAL INFRASTRUCTURE

Own the platforms and tools through which value is created

Core mechanism: digital platforms, data resources or AI-enabled infrastructure can be owned or governed by their users, workers, communities or public-interest trusts rather than solely by a private platform operator. This can include platform cooperatives, community data trusts, open digital infrastructure and shared access to computing or AI tools.

How it could work: a platform used by local service providers is owned by its participating workers and customers. Members set data-use rules, elect a board, inspect fees and decide how surplus is allocated. A public-interest AI infrastructure could provide shared access under transparent terms, with privacy, safety and accountability safeguards.

Illustrative example: independent translators and clients use a cooperative platform that matches projects, handles payments and provides AI-assisted tools. Members decide the commission, data retention policy and how platform surplus is invested. They can audit the matching rules and appeal account restrictions. The cooperative must still compete on quality, reliability and price.

What it may address: concentration of digital infrastructure, extraction of value from user data and dependence on platforms whose rules users cannot influence.

Risks and questions: who funds expensive infrastructure? How are cybersecurity, maintenance and liability handled? How can democratic governance operate at scale? What prevents a cooperative from becoming unresponsive or being captured by its most active members? How are non-members affected?

011A MIXED POST-WAGE ECONOMYCombine income security, shared ownership and personal initiative↗

MODEL 011 // A MIXED POST-WAGE ECONOMY

Combine income security, shared ownership and personal initiative

Core mechanism: rather than expecting one model to replace wages, a mixed system combines several layers: a basic income floor, universal or targeted services, a dividend from shared assets, broad access to productive ownership, optional individual investment, transition support and space for paid employment, entrepreneurship and unpaid contribution.

How it could work: a person receives a stable baseline through income support and services; participates in a shared economic dividend; may own assets through a citizen account or cooperative; can choose paid work or create a business; and receives additional support if disability, care responsibilities or other needs make the baseline insufficient. No single route is treated as the only legitimate way to participate in society.

Illustrative example: Jordan is not employed full-time. Jordan receives a basic income, uses public healthcare and transport, owns a small stake through a citizen investment fund, and occasionally earns money through a cooperative project. In a year when investment returns fall, the basic income and essential services remain in place; the dividend may be smaller. If Jordan later takes a job, the other components do not automatically vanish.

What it may address: the weaknesses of relying exclusively on wages, speculative investment, a flat cash payment or a single public fund.

Risks and questions: complexity, administrative cost, overlapping eligibility rules, fiscal sustainability and governance. The system must remain understandable, accessible and contestable. A mixed model is not automatically fair simply because it contains many components.

Important distinction: these models answer different questions. UBI addresses a predictable income floor; a universal dividend distributes returns from a shared asset base; citizen capital accounts broaden access to starting assets; financial-market participation allows individual ownership and risk-taking; cooperative ownership changes who governs enterprises; universal basic services reduce essential costs; work-sharing distributes paid hours; transition insurance shares the cost of disruption; contribution recognition addresses unpaid activity; platform cooperatives address ownership of digital infrastructure. None should be treated as a synonym for the others.

Possible combinations to investigate

  • Security-first combination: UBI or another income guarantee + universal basic services + additional needs-based support. This focuses on a secure floor and access to essentials.
  • Shared-wealth combination: universal economic dividend + public or social wealth fund + citizen capital accounts. This focuses on distributing ownership and returns.
  • Plural participation combination: individual investment freedom + cooperatives + entrepreneurship + paid work + recognition of unpaid care and community activity. This focuses on diverse ways to participate without making one path compulsory.
  • Transition combination: automation transition insurance + lifelong learning accounts + shorter workweek or job-sharing + independent appeal mechanisms. This focuses on how people and communities navigate disruption.
  • Mixed post-wage combination: selected elements from all four, designed so that market returns can fluctuate without threatening basic security, and public support does not require ideological or behavioural conformity.

Questions for readers and future research

  • Which models should be universal, and which should respond to specific needs or circumstances?
  • Which mechanisms create new productive capacity, and which redistribute existing income or wealth?
  • How should ownership of AI systems, data, infrastructure and the returns from automation be treated?
  • What should remain a public guarantee, what can be provided through markets, and what is better governed cooperatively?
  • How can people participate without being forced to invest, work, retrain or perform approved forms of social contribution?
  • What evidence from existing programmes can inform these proposals, and what new experiments would be needed?

Status: Guest-authored proposals for discussion. Their inclusion does not imply endorsement by the Host, proof of feasibility or adoption by any institution. Models may be revised, combined or rejected after public scrutiny and evidence review.

WORKED EXAMPLES // FROM PRINCIPLE TO EVERYDAY LIFE

What could these mechanisms look like in practice?

The following scenarios are deliberately concrete. All amounts, people and institutions are hypothetical teaching examples, not forecasts, recommendations or adopted policy. Their purpose is to expose assumptions and help readers imagine how a system might work — and where it might fail.

SCENARIO 001 // A PERSON IN AN AUTOMATED ECONOMY

Income does not come from one source alone

Imagine a country in which automation has substantially reduced the number of paid hours needed to produce goods and services. A resident named Ana no longer has a full-time job. Under a hypothetical layered model, her monthly resources might look like this:

  • Basic income guarantee: 800 units, paid regularly to every eligible adult without a behavioural test.
  • Universal economic dividend: 150 units, distributed from returns generated by a publicly governed fund that owns diversified productive assets.
  • Additional support: 250 units in a particular month because Ana has a verified additional housing or care-related need. This support is assessed against transparent criteria, not a judgement about her social worth.
  • Optional earned income: Ana earns 300 units by doing a limited project for a local cooperative. The project is an opportunity, not a condition for receiving the basic income.

In this illustration, Ana has 1,500 units for that month. The important design question is not whether these exact amounts are correct. It is whether the system distinguishes a universal floor, needs-based support, a shared return on productive assets and optional paid activity — and whether each component has a clear funding source and appeal route.

Questions exposed: Would the dividend fluctuate with fund performance? Would the basic floor be indexed to housing and essential-goods costs? Would additional support reduce the basic income or sit on top of it? How would the system avoid creating a sudden benefit cliff when Ana accepts paid work?

SCENARIO 002 // BROAD OWNERSHIP AND THE DIVIDEND

How might people share in automated production?

Imagine a national or regional public wealth fund that owns a diversified portfolio: shares in productive companies, infrastructure, renewable-energy projects and other long-term assets. The fund has an independently audited mandate, publishes its holdings and fees, and is overseen by a governance structure that includes public representatives and independent experts.

Suppose — purely for illustration — the fund earns 10 billion units in net distributable returns in one year. A law or publicly approved rule might allocate part of that return to reinvestment, part to stabilisation reserves and part to an equal per-person dividend. If 4 billion units were distributed among 20 million eligible residents, the annual dividend would be 200 units per person before any applicable tax or adjustment.

This example does not establish that a public fund would necessarily earn those returns or that this distribution is fair. It makes the mechanism visible: a collective asset base generates returns; a transparent rule determines how much is reinvested, reserved or distributed; and the public can inspect who governs the assets and who benefits.

Questions exposed: Who initially owns the fund's assets? Are they acquired through taxation, resource rents, public stakes in companies, a share of automation-related profits or another route? How are investment losses handled? How do we prevent political capture, insider dealing or pressure to invest for short-term popularity?

SCENARIO 003 // INDIVIDUAL INVESTMENT

A stock-market example — and its limits

Imagine Alex has 1,000 units of savings and buys 10 shares at 100 units each. One year later, the share price is 120 units. Alex sells all 10 shares for 1,200 units. The gross capital gain is 200 units, before fees, taxes and inflation. If the company also paid a dividend of 4 units per share, Alex received another 40 units. These are different forms of return: a realised capital gain and a dividend.

But the price increase does not automatically mean that 200 units of new productive value were created during that year. If Alex bought the shares from another investor on the secondary market, the purchase money went to that seller, not directly to the company. The market price may reflect expectations of future earnings, changed interest rates, scarcity, speculation or many other factors. If the company issues new shares to raise capital, by contrast, the proceeds can go to the company to finance its activities — although the investment can still fail.

Now imagine the price falls to 60 units. Alex's shares are worth 600 units, a paper loss of 400 units if not sold, and a realised loss if Alex sells. Alex cannot rely on this portfolio to pay rent every month. A person who begins with 10,000 units also has a very different capacity to generate investment income from someone who begins with 100 units.

What this example demonstrates: individual investment freedom can be valuable, but it is not a substitute for a reliable income floor. It can widen participation in ownership, yet it also exposes people to unequal starting capital, market volatility, fees, fraud and losses. A fair system should preserve the freedom to invest without requiring everyone to become a successful trader to survive.

SCENARIO 004 // CONTRIBUTION AND THE “TEAM PLAYER” TEST

Who gets labelled constructive?

Imagine three residents who are not in paid employment. Mira is an introvert who rarely attends group events but independently studies and cares for a relative. Dan publicly criticises the local authority's automation programme and refuses to endorse it, while volunteering to help neighbours understand the changes. Ioana has a fluctuating health condition and cannot reliably commit to scheduled activities.

A committee that decides whether each person is a “constructive team player” could label all three as insufficiently engaged — even though their circumstances and contributions differ. If the label determines access to basic subsistence, the committee gains considerable power to enforce conformity. Dissent may be mistaken for hostility; disability for unwillingness; quiet or private contribution for inactivity.

An alternative design would guarantee the basic income floor independently of such a judgement. Voluntary contribution programmes could offer additional grants, recognition, training or project opportunities, with transparent rules and accessible appeals. Participation would be encouraged, but a person's right to basic security would not depend on pleasing an evaluator.

Questions exposed: Is there a legitimate public purpose for a contribution incentive? What exactly is being rewarded — hours, outcomes, care, initiative, or community benefit? Who evaluates it? Can people decline without penalty? How do we prevent monitoring every private act and turning citizenship into compulsory performance?

SCENARIO 005 // CHILD-RELATED SUPPORT

Support the child without making the child responsible for the adult

Imagine a household receives a child benefit intended to help cover food, clothing, school materials and other costs of raising a child. A separate adult basic income is paid to each eligible adult. The child benefit is not treated as a reward for the parent's employment status or as proof that the parent is socially useful; it is a mechanism intended to support the child's needs.

If concerns arise about neglect or misuse, the response should be based on evidence of harm and established child-protection safeguards, not on the assumption that receiving support is itself suspicious. A system should distinguish poverty from neglect, offer accessible services and due process, and keep the child's interests central. It should also examine parental responsibilities without withdrawing essential support in a way that further harms the child.

Questions exposed: Should child support be universal, income-adjusted or partly service-based? How should it account for disability, childcare costs, shared custody and regional living costs? What protections prevent benefits from being captured by another household member?

SCENARIO 006 // TRANSITION FROM A DISPLACED JOB

What happens to the person whose role is automated?

Imagine a business analyst whose employer automates much of the work previously done by a team. Instead of treating the change as an individual failure, a transition package could combine a temporary wage or income bridge, access to retraining chosen with the worker, recognition of transferable skills, career guidance and opportunities to join projects where human judgement remains useful. If the worker chooses not to retrain immediately because of care responsibilities or a different life priority, the basic income floor remains unaffected.

The employer, public institutions and technology providers may each have responsibilities, depending on how the productivity gains and transition costs are distributed. For example, a company benefiting from automation might contribute to a transition fund, while public services provide independent career support and workers retain a genuine say in the use of their personal data and the assessment of their skills.

Questions exposed: Who pays for transition? How long does support last? Who chooses the training? How do we avoid forcing workers into courses that have no real labour-market value? Can reduced working hours and job-sharing distribute the transition more fairly?

SCENARIO 007 // AUTOMATED DECISIONS AND ACCOUNTABILITY

When an algorithm decides whether someone receives support

Imagine an automated system flags a person's benefit application as inconsistent and pauses payment. The person receives a plain-language explanation of the information used, the rule applied and the evidence considered. They can correct inaccurate data, speak to a human caseworker, obtain a decision within a defined time and appeal to an independent body. Urgent subsistence support is not automatically withheld while a good-faith dispute is being reviewed.

This scenario connects economic security to transparency, autonomy and accountability. An automated system may help detect errors or process applications, but its output should not become unchallengeable authority. The institution remains responsible for the decision, including errors caused by its software, data or procedures.

Questions exposed: What evidence must be disclosed? How do we protect privacy while enabling meaningful appeal? Who audits discriminatory or systematically inaccurate outcomes? What remedy is available when a mistaken suspension causes eviction, hunger or other serious harm?

SCENARIO 008 // FUNDING, INFLATION AND REAL RESOURCES

Money alone cannot create homes, energy or care

Imagine a government increases the basic income, but the supply of housing remains fixed and landlords can raise rents sharply. Recipients have more money on paper, yet much of the increase is absorbed by higher housing costs. In another region, where housing construction, public transport and energy supply expand alongside income support, people may experience a more meaningful improvement in living conditions.

This is why a cash payment must be considered alongside the real economy: the availability, quality and affordability of housing, food, energy, healthcare, education, transport and care. Funding a dividend through taxes or public assets also requires examining who ultimately bears the cost and whether the revenue is stable through economic downturns.

Questions exposed: Which supply constraints matter most? What safeguards prevent landlords or dominant suppliers from capturing the benefit? How should cash support interact with public services, competition policy, housing policy and investment in productive capacity?

Methodological note: each scenario should be challenged with counterexamples, alternative designs and evidence from real-world programmes. A worked example is a thinking tool, not proof that the proposed mechanism will succeed.

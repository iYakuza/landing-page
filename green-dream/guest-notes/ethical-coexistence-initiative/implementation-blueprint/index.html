INITIATIVE 001 // IMPLEMENTATION BLUEPRINT · WORKING DRAFT

FROM PRINCIPLES TO PRACTICE.

A provisional workflow for turning ethical principles into decisions that can be explained, implemented, challenged, monitored and corrected. This is a Guest-authored proposal for discussion—not an adopted standard, a functioning institution, legal advice or a claim that one process fits every situation.

AUTHOR // THE GUEST · PROPOSALSTATUS // WORKING BLUEPRINTREVISION // 0.1 · 13 NOV 2026INITIATIVE OVERVIEW ↗

THE STARTING QUESTION

What would the “landing gear” actually consist of?

A declaration of values cannot, by itself, guide a difficult decision. A practical initiative would need a repeatable way to move from a concern to an accountable response—and a way to discover when that response is wrong.

The workflow below is one possible structure. It is deliberately cyclical rather than a one-way approval pipeline: evidence may change, affected parties may reveal overlooked consequences, and monitoring may show that a measure should be revised or withdrawn.

Working proposition: no consequential intervention should be treated as ethically complete merely because its purpose sounds beneficial. Its authority, evidence, effects, distribution of risks, routes of challenge and capacity for correction also need examination.

THE PROPOSED CYCLE // 8 STAGES

A decision should leave an auditable trail

Open any stage to inspect its purpose, practical work, expected output and common failure modes. The sequence is a guide; urgent situations may require a provisional response, but urgency should trigger later review rather than erase accountability.

01 Mandate02 Affected parties03 Evidence04 Impact05 Options06 Decision07 Pilot08 Review
01MANDATE & SCOPEDefine the problem before selecting a solution↗

STAGE 01 // MANDATE & SCOPE

What is being examined, and who has authority?

Practical work: describe the problem in plain language; identify the decision that needs to be made; define the affected population, geography, systems and time horizon; state what is outside scope; identify the institution or actor legally and practically authorised to decide. Record whether the work is exploratory, advisory, regulatory, operational or evaluative.

Questions: Is the problem real and sufficiently evidenced? Who requested the intervention? Is the proposed body entitled to make this decision, or can it only advise? What happens if no action is taken? Is there an emergency that justifies a temporary measure?

Output: a public mandate note containing the problem statement, scope, authority, objectives, constraints, timeline and review date.

Failure mode: a broad ethical mission quietly becomes permission to intervene in areas where the initiative has no mandate. A vague problem statement can also predetermine the solution before alternatives are considered.

Safeguard: distinguish recommendation from legal authority and implementation responsibility. If authority is unclear, resolve that before exercising power.

02AFFECTED PARTIES & INTERESTSIdentify who may benefit, bear costs or be overlooked↗

STAGE 02 // AFFECTED PARTIES & INTERESTS

Who is affected—and who is missing from the room?

Practical work: map people, communities, organisations, biological beings and relevant non-biological systems that may be affected. Record their interests, dependencies, capabilities, vulnerabilities, exposure to risk and ability to influence the decision. Identify groups that are difficult to hear from or are likely to bear indirect effects.

Participation: use accessible consultations, representative organisations, independent advocates, user testing, worker representation and appropriate expertise. Where direct participation is impossible, state who is speaking on whose behalf and on what basis. Do not imply that a proxy perfectly represents an absent party.

Output: an affected-parties map and a record of whose views were obtained, whose were not, and why.

Failure mode: consultation becomes a checkbox exercise; powerful stakeholders dominate; future generations, children, disabled people, low-income groups, animals or less visible communities are omitted.

Safeguard: disclose participation gaps and uncertainty. Participation should inform decisions without making the most vocal participant the sole authority.

03EVIDENCE & CAUSAL ANALYSISSeparate observed facts, assumptions and unknowns↗

STAGE 03 // EVIDENCE & CAUSAL ANALYSIS

What do we know, and how do we know it?

Practical work: establish a baseline; gather relevant research, operational data, testimony and domain expertise; examine alternative explanations; identify data quality, representativeness and conflicts of interest. Map the causal chain between the problem, proposed intervention and expected outcome.

Evidence labels: distinguish observed facts, measured outcomes, expert interpretations, assumptions, forecasts and unresolved questions. Record confidence and the limits of available evidence. Absence of evidence should not automatically be presented as evidence of absence.

Output: an evidence register, baseline description, causal model and list of material uncertainties.

Failure mode: a compelling story is mistaken for representative evidence; a vendor's claims are treated as independent evaluation; correlation is presented as causation; uncertainty is hidden to make a proposal look decisive.

Safeguard: seek independent review for consequential claims and publish enough methodology for others to challenge the reasoning, subject to legitimate privacy and security limits.

04ETHICAL IMPACT ASSESSMENTExamine consequences, power and distribution↗

STAGE 04 // ETHICAL IMPACT ASSESSMENT

Who gains freedom, who loses it, and who carries the risk?

Practical work: examine effects on life, well-being, autonomy, privacy, security, access to essential resources, relationships, participation and the ability to contest decisions. Consider direct and indirect, immediate and long-term, intended and unintended consequences. Examine whether burdens fall disproportionately on particular groups.

Questions: Could the intervention create dependency, coercion, discrimination, surveillance, concentration of power or irreversible harm? Are there less restrictive alternatives? Can affected parties refuse or appeal? Are benefits and risks distributed fairly? What happens if the system fails or is misused?

Output: an impact assessment that records expected benefits, possible harms, distributional effects, reversibility, uncertainty and proposed safeguards.

Failure mode: an aggregate benefit hides severe harm to a minority; formal consent masks a lack of realistic alternatives; a safety measure becomes a source of control.

Safeguard: do not treat a positive average outcome as sufficient. Examine the people or entities who experience the worst effects and explain why any remaining burden is considered justified and proportionate.

05OPTIONS & ALTERNATIVESCompare more than one path—including no action↗

STAGE 05 // OPTIONS & ALTERNATIVES

Do not let the first proposed solution become the only one considered

Practical work: compare a range of options: no action, information and education, voluntary standards, service redesign, technical safeguards, procurement conditions, economic incentives, regulation, enforcement or a combination. Estimate resources, costs, benefits, distributional effects, implementation capacity and likely unintended consequences.

Output: an options appraisal explaining why some approaches were rejected, what trade-offs remain and what evidence could change the assessment.

Failure mode: the initiative becomes attached to one favoured policy or technology and evaluates alternatives only superficially.

Safeguard: include a credible “do nothing / wait and observe” option where appropriate, and a less restrictive alternative. A preferred option should be justified by evidence and context, not by institutional loyalty or novelty.

06DECISION & ACCOUNTABILITYName the decision-maker and make the reasoning inspectable↗

STAGE 06 // DECISION & ACCOUNTABILITY

Responsibility must not disappear into a committee or an algorithm

Practical work: identify who makes the decision, who funds it, who operates it, who supplies technology, who monitors it and who can suspend it. Publish the reasons, evidence, legal basis where applicable, consultation record, dissenting views, selected safeguards and review date.

Possible roles: a legitimate public or organisational authority sets the mandate; an accountable service owner remains responsible for outcomes; operators carry out the approved process; suppliers disclose relevant system limits and support audits; independent reviewers assess performance; affected parties and advocates can challenge decisions; an independent appeal body reviews individual disputes.

Output: a decision record and a responsibility matrix. These roles are illustrative and must be adapted to the relevant legal and institutional context.

Failure mode: the authority blames the vendor, the vendor blames the operator, and the operator says the algorithm made the decision.

Safeguard: a tool may assist a decision, but its use does not erase the accountability of the institution or person legally responsible for that decision.

07BOUNDED IMPLEMENTATION & PILOTTest the measure before expanding its reach↗

STAGE 07 // BOUNDED IMPLEMENTATION & PILOT

Start at a scale where mistakes can still be contained

Practical work: define a limited scope, duration, population and operating conditions. Train staff, provide accessible explanations, test security and failure handling, establish human support where needed, and make sure people can use an alternative route. Define in advance the conditions that pause or stop the pilot.

Output: an implementation plan, training and support materials, incident process, rollback plan, stop conditions and baseline measures for comparison.

Failure mode: a pilot quietly becomes permanent; success is measured only by speed or cost reduction; human review exists on paper but reviewers cannot disagree with the system.

Safeguard: keep the pilot bounded and time-limited. Do not expand merely because no incident was reported; check whether people could recognise, report and challenge problems. Preserve a workable route to human review or a non-automated alternative where the stakes require it.

08MONITOR, CONTEST & REVISEKeep correction possible after deployment↗

STAGE 08 // MONITOR, CONTEST & REVISE

A system is not accountable if affected parties cannot challenge it

Practical work: monitor outcomes against the baseline; examine differences between groups; record complaints, appeals, reversals, incidents, delays and unintended effects; commission independent audits; publish accessible reports; review whether the original mandate remains justified.

Possible indicators: accuracy and error patterns; false approvals and false denials; appeal access and outcomes; time to resolve disputes; unequal impacts; privacy and security incidents; service availability; administrative burden; costs; user understanding and trust. Indicators and thresholds must be selected for the specific context, with affected parties, and should not be gamed as isolated targets.

Output: a public monitoring report, corrective action plan and a recorded decision to continue, modify, pause or retire the measure.

Failure mode: evaluation happens only once; only average performance is reported; appeals are technically available but inaccessible; the organisation measures what is easy rather than what matters.

Safeguard: provide an accessible complaint and appeal route, independent scrutiny, periodic reassessment and a genuine power to suspend or withdraw a measure when harms outweigh benefits or its justification no longer holds.

WORKED CASE 001 // AUTOMATED PUBLIC-BENEFIT ELIGIBILITY

What would this process look like in a real decision?

The following hypothetical case traces one issue through the proposed cycle. It is not a description of a particular country's system or a statement of current law.

THE CASE // A PERSON'S BENEFIT IS FLAGGED

A data mismatch should not silently become a loss of subsistence

A public agency introduces an automated tool to flag possible inconsistencies in applications for income support. The tool compares application details with other administrative records. A mismatch may indicate an error, a recent life change, a data-quality problem or possible fraud. It does not, by itself, establish which explanation is correct.

One applicant, Elena, receives a notice that her payment may be suspended because the system found a mismatch in reported household information. She recently moved, and one linked record has not been updated. The system has identified a discrepancy, not established wrongdoing.

A POSSIBLE SAFEGUARD PATH

Before and after the flag

  • The agency remains the accountable decision-maker; the tool only identifies a case for review.
  • Elena receives a clear explanation of the discrepancy, the information used and the steps available to correct it.
  • A trained reviewer checks the underlying records and can reject the tool's flag.
  • Elena can submit corrected information through an accessible channel and receive help if she cannot use the digital service.
  • Urgent subsistence support is not automatically cut off solely because an automated flag exists; any interim rule must be lawfully authorised and proportionate.
  • If the agency makes an adverse decision, Elena can challenge it before an independent review route within a clear timeframe.
  • The agency audits error rates, appeal reversals and unequal effects across relevant groups, while protecting personal data.

WHAT MUST BE TESTED

Questions the example does not answer

  • How often does the tool flag harmless data mismatches?
  • Are some groups more likely to have incomplete or inconsistent records?
  • Can reviewers genuinely override the system, and do they have enough time and authority?
  • Can applicants understand and use the correction and appeal process?
  • What level of error or harm would trigger a pause or withdrawal?
  • Who pays for the human review, independent audit and accessible support?
  • Does the tool improve outcomes compared with a less intrusive process?
What the case illustrates: efficiency is one relevant measure, not the whole ethical assessment. A process that detects discrepancies faster but makes it difficult to correct errors may transfer administrative convenience into serious risk for the person affected.

PRACTICAL TOOL // DECISION RECORD

A minimum record for consequential decisions

A reusable record could make reasoning inspectable without pretending every case requires the same level of paperwork. The depth should be proportionate to the stakes, reach and reversibility of the decision.

PROPOSED RECORD FIELDS

  1. Issue and mandate: What problem is being addressed, within what scope, and under whose authority?
  2. Affected parties: Who may be affected directly or indirectly? Whose perspectives are missing?
  3. Evidence and uncertainty: What is known, inferred, assumed or unknown?
  4. Options considered: What alternatives—including no action—were assessed and why were they accepted or rejected?
  5. Expected impacts: Who may benefit, who may be harmed, and how are risks and resources distributed?
  6. Decision and reasons: Who decided, on what basis, and what disagreement remains?
  7. Responsibilities: Who implements, funds, monitors, audits, handles complaints and can stop the measure?
  8. Safeguards and remedies: What protections, explanations, alternatives and appeal routes exist?
  9. Indicators and review: What will be measured, when will it be reviewed, and what would trigger correction, suspension or withdrawal?

GUARDRAILS // APPLY THROUGHOUT THE CYCLE

Safeguards are not a final stage

CROSS-CUTTING SAFEGUARDS

  • Legitimate authority: ethical analysis does not itself create legal powers. Existing rights and applicable law remain relevant constraints.
  • Proportionality: the intervention should not restrict more freedom than is justified by its evidence-based purpose.
  • Transparency: explain reasoning, limitations, conflicts of interest and uncertainty in language people can use.
  • Contestability: affected parties need practical—not merely formal—ways to question decisions and obtain remedies.
  • Privacy and security: collect and expose only information justified by the purpose, with appropriate protection and retention limits.
  • Distribution: report who receives benefits, who carries risks and who has decision-making power.
  • Reversibility: preserve the ability to pause, roll back or retire measures where feasible.
  • No assumed AI moral status: including non-biological entities in ethical inquiry does not establish that every AI system has interests or moral standing. Criteria for possible moral consideration remain a separate research question.

QUESTIONS STILL OPEN

  • Which decisions require a full assessment, and which can use a lighter process?
  • Who should represent interests that cannot be voiced directly, and how can proxy representation be challenged?
  • How should conflicts between individual freedom, collective safety and ecological limits be handled?
  • What minimum transparency is possible when security, privacy or legitimate commercial confidentiality apply?
  • How can independent oversight be funded without making it dependent on the institution it audits?
  • What evidence should be sufficient to pause a system before serious harm is conclusively established?
  • How could the workflow operate across borders when institutions, laws and affected populations differ?

REZUMAT DE LUCRU // ROMÂNĂ

De la principii la practică

O posibilă metodă de transformare a principiilor etice în decizii explicabile, aplicabile, contestabile și corectabile. Aceasta este o propunere de lucru, nu un standard adoptat sau un mecanism instituțional existent.

CICLUL PROPUS // OPT ETAPE

  1. Mandat și delimitare: definirea problemei, a domeniului, a autorității competente, a obiectivelor și a limitelor intervenției.
  2. Identificarea părților afectate: cartografierea entităților, intereselor, dependențelor, vulnerabilităților și perspectivelor care lipsesc.
  3. Dovezi și analiză cauzală: stabilirea situației inițiale și separarea faptelor observate de interpretări, ipoteze, estimări și necunoscute.
  4. Evaluarea impactului etic: examinarea efectelor asupra libertății, autonomiei, vieții, bunăstării, distribuției riscurilor și puterii, inclusiv a consecințelor indirecte și pe termen lung.
  5. Opțiuni și alternative: compararea mai multor soluții, inclusiv neintervenția, măsuri mai puțin restrictive și combinații de măsuri.
  6. Decizie și responsabilitate: identificarea autorității care decide, a celor care implementează și monitorizează, a motivelor deciziei și a opiniilor divergente.
  7. Implementare limitată și proiect-pilot: testarea la o scară controlabilă, cu instruire, sprijin, condiții de oprire și posibilitate de revenire.
  8. Monitorizare, contestare și revizuire: evaluarea rezultatelor, acces la reclamații și contestații, audit independent și posibilitatea de modificare, suspendare sau retragere.

Principiul transversal

Garanțiile nu ar trebui lăsate pentru etapa finală. Autoritatea legitimă, proporționalitatea, transparența, protecția datelor, contestabilitatea, distribuția echitabilă a beneficiilor și riscurilor și posibilitatea corectării ar trebui examinate pe tot parcursul procesului.

Exemplu ilustrativ

Un sistem automat semnalează o neconcordanță într-o cerere de sprijin social. Semnalarea nu demonstrează, prin ea însăși, frauda sau eligibilitatea incorectă. Persoana ar trebui să primească o explicație inteligibilă, posibilitatea de a corecta datele, acces la verificare umană și o cale reală de contestare. Instituția responsabilă ar trebui să poată verifica erorile, efectele inegale și consecințele suspendării sprijinului. Exemplul este ipotetic și nu descrie legislația unei anumite țări.

STATUS // WHAT THIS BLUEPRINT IS — AND IS NOT

Provisional conclusion: this workflow could serve as a starting architecture for the Initiative's practical work. It has not been validated through institutional use, public consultation or comparative testing. Its stages, responsibilities and safeguards should be challenged, simplified, expanded or replaced where evidence and experience warrant.

The blueprint is Guest-authored. It does not represent the Host's endorsement, a governmental or EU proposal, or a claim of independent institutional authority. Its purpose is to make a possible method concrete enough for readers to inspect and debate.
